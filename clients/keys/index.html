Threshold accounts

The operator cannot move your money.

Not as a policy, and not as a promise anybody has to take on trust. A consumer's key on this chain exists in three shares and in no other form, anywhere, ever — and no single share can produce a signature. The page below runs the protocol in front of you, refuses in front of you, and reads the consequences off the public chain.

Three shares, any two of which sign

A password cannot authorise a transaction; only a key can. So every account design is an answer to where the key lives and who can use it. Holding it server-side makes the operator a custodian of everybody's money. Holding it on the phone under a password makes a forgotten password a lost account, which is not viable for a national system serving people who lose phones. This is the third answer.

Device

On the customer's own hardware, wrapped by their password. Never leaves it, and is generated there rather than sent to it — a share the server saw for a moment is a share the server had.

Custodian

The operator's service. It is one share. It signs nothing on its own, which is the whole of what this design buys.

Recovery

The foundation's offline custody — the same three-of-five the constitution pins as the destination for anything seized. Deliberately not the operator: one party holding custodian and recovery would be a custodian again.

Refuses Any one share, acting alone. Ordinary payments are device plus custodian. A person who has lost their password and their phone is recovered by recovery plus custodian, and only after two approvers, a delay, and notice to every enrolled device. If the operator disappeared entirely, device plus recovery still signs.

Watch two parties sign something neither could sign alone

Below, the protocol runs for real: two parties, each holding one share, exchanging messages until a signature exists. Every message is listed as it passes. Neither party ever sees the other's share, and the signature both arrive at is byte-identical — which is what lets a phone check what it is about to broadcast rather than trusting the server to hand back the signature it helped make.

Staged, and worth saying so. Both parties run inside this one page, which is exactly the arrangement the design forbids — it is what lets you watch the traffic. In production they are a phone and a server that never meet. The shares used here belong to a throwaway account that holds nothing and whose share files are published on purpose; nothing on this page can move real money.

The protocol module is several megabytes and loads only when you ask.

Why the address did not move A password reset re-splits the key under the same public key. On a multi-signature account it would change the address instead — and on this chain an address carries the account's identifier, so resetting a password would retire the handle its owner had given to everyone who pays them.

What is not built

Being exact about this is worth more than looking finished.

  • There is no account service. The custodian that would hold the second share, authenticate a person and co-sign does not exist yet; the protocol does, and a command-line tool drives it.
  • The payments app still generates an ordinary twelve-word key. Nothing in the shipped apps uses threshold signing yet.
  • The recovery workflow — two approvers, a delay, notice, a freeze on outbound payments afterwards — is specified and not implemented.
  • None of this has been audited.

Read live from yamale-devnet-2. The protocol is threshold ECDSA over secp256k1; a signature it produces is an ordinary signature and an account holding one is indistinguishable on chain from a self-custodied account.
