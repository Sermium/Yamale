Threshold accounts

The operator cannot move your money.

Not as a policy, and not as a promise anybody has to take on trust. A consumer's key on this chain exists in three shares and in no other form, anywhere, ever — and no single share can produce a signature. The page below runs the protocol in front of you, refuses in front of you, and reads the consequences off the public chain.

Reading the chain.

Three shares, any two of which sign

A password cannot authorise a transaction; only a key can. So every account design is an answer to where the key lives and who can use it. Holding it server-side makes the operator a custodian of everybody's money. Holding it on the phone under a password makes a forgotten password a lost account, which is not viable for a national system serving people who lose phones. This is the third answer.

Device

On the customer's own hardware, wrapped by their password. Never leaves it, and is generated there rather than sent to it — a share the server saw for a moment is a share the server had.

Custodian

The operator's service. It is one share. It signs nothing on its own, which is the whole of what this design buys.

Recovery

The foundation's offline custody — the same three-of-five the constitution pins as the destination for anything seized. Deliberately not the operator: one party holding custodian and recovery would be a custodian again.

Any two of them, and which two says what for

Three shares make three pairs, and each pair is a different situation. This was one forty-six word sentence, which is the whole mechanism in a place nobody reads it twice.

These twoSignWhen
Device + Custodian Ordinary payments Every day. The phone asks, the service co-signs, and neither could have sent it alone.
Recovery + Custodian A reset that keeps the address Password and phone both lost — and only after two approvers, a delay, and notice to every enrolled device.
Device + Recovery Everything If the operator disappeared entirely. This is the edge that makes the custodian optional rather than trusted.
Any one, alone Nothing Always. This row is the product — the three above are what it buys.
The custodian appears in two of the three pairs and is required by none of them.

Watch two parties sign something neither could sign alone

Below, the protocol runs for real: two parties, each holding one share, exchanging messages until a signature exists. Every message is listed as it passes. Neither party ever sees the other's share, and the signature both arrive at is byte-identical — which is what lets a phone check what it is about to broadcast rather than trusting the server to hand back the signature it helped make.

Staged, and worth saying so. Both parties run inside this one page, which is exactly the arrangement the design forbids — it is what lets you watch the traffic. In production they are a phone and a server that never meet. The shares used here belong to a throwaway account that holds nothing and whose share files are published on purpose; nothing on this page can move real money.

The protocol module is several megabytes and loads only when you ask.

Why the address did not move A password reset re-splits the key under the same public key. On a multi-signature account it would change the address instead — and on this chain an address carries the account's identifier, so resetting a password would retire the handle its owner had given to everyone who pays them.

Taking your key, and what it costs you

The claim above has an obvious price, and a page that made the claim without naming it would be selling half a story. If no single share can sign, can the person whose money it is ever hold their own key?

Yes. It is theirs. But there is no seed phrase to hand over — the key was never written down, as words or otherwise, and has never existed in one piece. What leaves is the key itself, assembled for the first time, on the holder's own device.

It will not be twenty-four words, and nobody should accept twenty-four words from anyone claiming to be us. BIP39 words are entropy fed through a derivation: a phrase encoding these key bytes would import into any ordinary wallet and arrive at a different key at a different address. The holder would see an empty account and conclude the platform had lost their money. So what is handed over is a private key, labelled as one.

Ask, wait, ask again

Three steps, every one of them signed by the account itself. The operator cannot request a disclosure on somebody's behalf, cannot refuse one, and cannot shorten the wait for a favoured customer or lengthen it for an inconvenient one — the delay is a chain parameter, not a setting in the platform's software.

The waiting period exists for one person: the holder whose phone was taken an hour ago. Once a key is out nothing protects them — a freeze does not bind a key somebody holds in plaintext, and a re-share produces new shares under the same public key, so a disclosed key signs for ever. Time plus notice is the only protection left, and every enrolled device is told the moment a request is made.

What ends

  • Anyone who sees the key owns the account. A screenshot, a synced note, a support agent asking for a paste, malware on the machine it is opened on. There is no second factor behind it any more.
  • A stolen key cannot be cancelled. The account can be re-keyed, but re-keying deliberately keeps the same address — so the exported key keeps working. There is no revocation; the only escape is to move everything to a new account.
  • The custody guarantee ends. The platform keeps its share and goes on co-signing, so the account still works and nothing is taken away. What ends is the guarantee: two-of-three is no longer a property anybody can rely on, because one of the three is now sufficient on its own for whoever holds the exported key. Keeping the share is a convenience, not a protection, and the acknowledgement says so.
  • The account is marked, publicly and permanently. Anyone can ask whether a given account's key has been disclosed. Some counterparties may decline to deal with one that has.

Read the acknowledgement in full. It is the exact text whose hash is recorded on the chain when somebody confirms — which is what makes "they agreed to version 1" a claim anybody can check rather than one they must take on trust.

What is not built

Being exact about this is worth more than looking finished.

  • There is no account service. The custodian that would hold the second share, authenticate a person and co-sign does not exist yet; the protocol does, and a command-line tool drives it.
  • The payments app still generates an ordinary twelve-word key. Nothing in the shipped apps uses threshold signing yet.
  • The recovery workflow — two approvers, a delay, notice, a freeze on outbound payments afterwards — is specified and not implemented.
  • None of this has been audited.

Read live from yamale-devnet-2. The protocol is threshold ECDSA over secp256k1; a signature it produces is an ordinary signature and an account holding one is indistinguishable on chain from a self-custodied account.
